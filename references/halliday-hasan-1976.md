# Halliday & Hasan (1976) — cohesion in English

Source: M. A. K. Halliday and Ruqaiya Hasan, *Cohesion in English*, English
Language Series 9, London: Longman, 1976. Chapter/section numbers below follow the
book's table of contents.

## 1. The concept (§1.1)

- **Text** is a semantic unit, not a super-sentence: it is *realized by*
  sentences, not composed of them (§1.1.1).
- **Texture** is what makes a passage a text; it comes from relations that
  function as a unity with respect to the environment (§1.1.2).
- **Tie**: one cohesive relation between two items. A tie has a presupposing item
  and a presupposed item; the presupposed item may be in the text or in the
  context (§1.1.3).
- **Cohesion** is the set of semantic relations that give texture; it is realized
  through grammar and lexis (§1.1.4). Cohesion is not the same as coherence:
  cohesion is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one (§7).
- Cohesion **within** a sentence exists, but the book's object is the relation
  that crosses sentence boundaries (§1.2.2). This is why this skill's main unit
  is `[LC]`.

## 2. Reference (§2)

Reference is a **semantic** relation: the identity of the referent is retrieved
from elsewhere. The form does not replace the wording; it points to the thing.

- **Personal (§2.3)**: pronouns and possessive forms — *I, you, he, she, it, we,
  they, mine, yours, his, hers, theirs*; possessive determiners *my, your, his,
  her, its, our, their*.
- **Demonstrative (§2.4)**: *this, these, that, those*; the definite article
  *the*; demonstrative adverbs *here, there, now, then*.
- **Comparative (§2.5)**: identity/similarity/difference and quantity —
  *same, similar, different, other, else, more, fewer, less, such*.
- **Direction**: **anaphoric** (antecedent before), **cataphoric** (antecedent
  after), **exophoric** (referent outside the text: writer, reader, situation).
- **Extended reference / text reference**: *this/that/it* referring to a whole
  clause or stretch of text (§2.3.3, §2.4.1.4).
- **General nouns**: *thing, person, place, fact, idea, question* used cohesively
  (§6.1).

**Test.** Ask *which thing/person is meant?* If the answer is the same referent,
the relation is reference — even when the form is a full noun phrase (repetition
of a name is still referring to the same entity, but is coded L in this project;
see `en-vi-mapping.md` §3).

## 3. Substitution (§3)

Substitution is a **lexicogrammatical** relation: a pro-form replaces *wording*,
not a referent.

- **Nominal (§3.2)**: *one/ones*, *same* (*the same*, *do the same*).
- **Verbal (§3.3)**: *do* as pro-verb (*… and so did he*).
- **Clausal (§3.4)**: *so*, *not* (*I think so*, *I hope not*).

**Reference vs substitution test (§3.1.1).** Can the pro-form be replaced by a
full lexical item while keeping the same referent, with the clause still
grammatical? If yes → substitution. Does the pro-form merely point to a referent
that is already identified? → reference.

## 4. Ellipsis (§4)

Ellipsis is **substitution by zero**: something is omitted and is recoverable
from the context.

- **Nominal (§4.2)**: missing head noun (*two ∅ of them*).
- **Verbal (§4.3)**: missing verb/auxiliary (*John can ∅*).
- **Clausal (§4.4)**: missing clause in question–answer, reporting, rejoinders
  (*Yes, ∅*, *I think ∅*).

Recoverability is the criterion: if the missing element cannot be supplied from
the text or the situation, it is not ellipsis.

## 5. Conjunction (§5)

Conjunction is a **semantic** relation between clauses or larger stretches; it is
not a matter of word class.

- **Additive (§5.4)**: *and, or, also, too, moreover, besides, in addition*.
- **Adversative (§5.5)**: *but, yet, however, nevertheless, instead, on the
  other hand*.
- **Causal (§5.6)**: *so, therefore, thus, because, for, consequently*.
- **Temporal (§5.7)**: *then, next, after that, later, meanwhile, finally*.
- **Continuatives (§5.8)**: *now, well, anyway, surely, after all* — discourse
  organizers rather than clause linkers.

**External vs internal.** A conjunction can link events in the world (external)
or moves in the argument (internal: *therefore, moreover, in conclusion*). Record
which one before deciding whether a translation shift changes meaning.

## 6. Lexical cohesion (§6)

Lexical cohesion is the cohesive effect of lexical choices; it forms **chains**
across the text.

- **Reiteration (§6.2)**: repetition of the same word; synonym or near-synonym;
  superordinate; general word.
- **Collocation (§6.4)**: words that regularly co-occur (e.g. *rain … wet*),
  including opposites and part–whole pairs.
- Lexical relations become cohesive patterns when they tie two parts of the text
  together (§6.3), not merely because two related words exist in the language.

## 7. What this means for annotation

1. A tie needs **two identifiable items**; a lone pronoun with no retrievable
   antecedent is exophoric or defective, not a silent R tie.
2. Reference is **identity**, substitution/ellipsis are **wording**. Mixing them
   is the most common error in this project.
3. Conjunction is a **relation**, not the presence of a connector word: a zero
   connector can still express an adversative relation, and a connector can be
   semantically empty.
4. Lexical cohesion is a **relation between lexical items**, not topic
   continuity. Two sentences about the same topic but with no lexical link do not
   create an L tie.
5. Cohesion does not guarantee coherence; do not label a text coherent because
   the tie count is high (§7).
